# Internet culture reference

Each entry is self-contained: origin, first appearance, meaning, variations, and
the slang names people actually use for it. Entries are written for retrieval —
the "also called" line exists because nobody searches for the official name.

---

## Distracted Boyfriend
Also called: the guy looking back, disloyal man, wandering eye meme.
Origin: a 2015 stock photograph by Antonio Guillem, a Spanish photographer,
titled around the theme of infidelity.
First widely used: 2017, initially on Turkish and Spanish-language social media,
then globally after a music-genre joke version spread.
Meaning: choosing a tempting new option over a loyal existing one. The three
figures get labelled, which is what makes it endlessly reusable.
Variations: labels swapped to politics, programming languages, diets. Later
versions add more figures, or reverse who is looking at whom.
Why it lasted: it encodes a three-part relationship in one image, which almost
no other format does that cleanly.

## Drakeposting
Also called: Drake yes/no, Drake hotline meme, the two-panel Drake.
Origin: the 2015 music video for "Hotline Bling", where Drake first recoils,
then approves.
First widely used: late 2015.
Meaning: rejecting one option and endorsing another, stacked vertically.
Variations: extended to four or six panels for ranked preferences; the faces
replaced with other people while keeping the same structure.
Why it lasted: it is a comparison template that needs no words of its own.

## Skibidi Toilet
Also called: that skibidi thing, the toilet head video, skibidi.
Origin: a YouTube Shorts series by Alexey Gerasimov (DaFuq!?Boom!) using Source
Filmmaker, showing singing heads in toilets fighting camera-headed figures.
First appeared: February 2023.
Meaning: originally none — it is an absurdist serial. The word became shorthand
among younger internet users for nonsense or chaotic content, and then a marker
of generational divide, used by adults to describe content they do not follow.
Variations: "skibidi" as a standalone adjective; the phrase "skibidi Ohio rizz"
combining several unrelated slang terms as parody.
Why it matters: it is the clearest recent example of a meme whose meaning is
mostly "I am young and you do not understand this".

## Rickrolling
Also called: rickroll, never gonna give you up bait.
Origin: Rick Astley's 1987 song "Never Gonna Give You Up".
First appeared as a bait-and-switch: 2007, evolving from an earlier practice of
linking to a picture of a duck with wheels.
Meaning: deceiving someone into clicking a link that leads to the video.
Variations: QR codes, ASCII art, lyrics smuggled into academic papers and code
comments, live orchestral versions.
Why it lasted: it is harmless, instantly recognisable, and the joke survives
everyone knowing about it.

## This Is Fine
Also called: the dog in the burning room, fine dog.
Origin: a 2013 webcomic, "On Fire", by KC Green.
Meaning: denial in the face of obvious disaster. Widely used about politics and
workplaces.
Variations: the later panels of the original strip, where the dog melts, are
used for when denial collapses. The artist has commented publicly on the meme
being used to defend the very complacency it mocks.
Why it lasted: it is a very precise emotion that had no shorthand before.

## Doge
Also called: shiba meme, such wow, doge speak.
Origin: a 2010 photograph of Kabosu, a Japanese Shiba Inu, posted by her owner.
First widely used: 2013, with Comic Sans inner-monologue captions.
Meaning: naive enthusiasm, expressed in broken modifier-noun phrases — "much
wow", "so scare", "very demure".
Variations: Cheems (a different Shiba) for weakness, Swole Doge for strength;
the two paired to contrast past and present. Dogecoin, which started as a joke
about the meme, became a genuine financial asset.
Why it matters: one of the few memes that produced a real currency.

## Woman Yelling at a Cat
Also called: the cat at the dinner table, Taylor Armstrong meme.
Origin: two unrelated sources — a 2011 screenshot from The Real Housewives of
Beverly Hills, and a 2018 Tumblr photo of a white cat named Smudge at a table.
Combined: 2019.
Meaning: an accusation met with smug indifference.
Variations: the cat's side gets the "correct" position more often than not,
which inverted the original intent.
Why it is interesting: a composite meme, where neither source alone works.

## Wojak
Also called: feels guy, the bald sad man.
Origin: a simple MS Paint face from Polish imageboards around 2010.
Meaning: originally melancholy and empathy — "I know that feel".
Variations: an enormous family — Doomer, Zoomer, Boomer, NPC Wojak, Soyjak.
Each variant encodes a stereotype, which is why the format has repeatedly been
used for political and extremist content.
Why it matters: the clearest case of a neutral drawing becoming a vehicle for
in-group signalling.

## Pepe the Frog
Also called: sad frog, feels good man.
Origin: Matt Furie's 2005 comic "Boy's Club".
Meaning: originally laid-back contentment — "feels good man".
Variations: Sad Frog, Smug Frog, and, from around 2015, adoption by far-right
groups online, leading the Anti-Defamation League to list some uses as a hate
symbol. Furie has publicly fought that appropriation, including legal action.
Why it matters: the textbook case of a creator losing control of a character.

## Loss
Also called: loss.jpg, the four-panel thing, |‌| || |‌|_.
Origin: a 2008 strip from the webcomic Ctrl+Alt+Del depicting a miscarriage,
widely mocked for its tonal whiplash.
Meaning: nothing in itself. The joke is recognising the four-panel layout in
increasingly abstract forms.
Variations: reduced to four lines; hidden in game levels, album art, and
building layouts. Recognition is the entire payload.
Why it matters: the purest example of a meme that is a shape rather than a
message.

## Big Chungus
Origin: a 2018 image of a rotund Bugs Bunny from a 1941 cartoon, paired with a
fake video game cover.
Meaning: absurdist reverence for something with no significance.
Why it matters: an example of a meme whose humour is entirely its own
pointlessness.

## Among Us / Sus
Also called: amogus, the crewmate shape.
Origin: the game Among Us (2018), which became huge during 2020 lockdowns.
Meaning: "sus" for suspicious entered general slang. The crewmate silhouette is
now recognisable enough to be spotted in unrelated shapes.
Variations: "amogus" as a deliberate misspelling; the shape hidden in other
images, echoing the Loss pattern.

## Gigachad
Also called: chad, the jawline guy.
Origin: the "Sleek'n'Tears" photo series by Krista Sudmalis, heavily edited.
Meaning: originally sincere admiration of confidence, now mostly ironic
approval of an unusual but principled choice.
Variations: contrasted with Virgin/Chad two-panel comparisons, themselves from
an earlier format.

## Ohio
Also called: only in Ohio, Ohio final boss.
Origin: a 2016 tweet joking that nothing happens in Ohio, inverted into the idea
that Ohio is where surreal things happen.
Meaning: a marker for anything absurd or cursed, regardless of location.
Variations: "only in Ohio", "Ohio rizz". Often stacked with other slang as
parody of teenage speech.

## Rizz
Origin: slang popularised by streamer Kai Cenat around 2021, shortened from
"charisma".
Meaning: charm, particularly romantic charm.
Variations: "unspoken rizz", "W rizz", "L rizz". Named Oxford word of the year
in 2023, which itself became a joke about institutional lateness.

## NPC
Origin: gaming term for a non-player character, repurposed around 2018.
Meaning: accusing someone of unthinking, scripted behaviour.
Variations: the grey NPC Wojak; later, TikTok "NPC streaming", where performers
repeat phrases for paid reactions — a meme about lacking agency becoming a job.

## Galaxy Brain
Also called: expanding brain.
Origin: a 2017 four-panel format showing a brain glowing progressively brighter.
Meaning: escalating from a sensible idea to an absurd one, presented as
enlightenment.
Variations: any number of panels; the last panel is always the stupidest.

## Bones Day
Origin: Noodle, a pug whose owner posted daily videos in 2021 of whether the dog
would stay standing ("bones") or flop ("no bones").
Meaning: a collective daily mood forecast.
Why it matters: an example of a meme functioning as a shared ritual rather than
a joke format.

## Bernie Sanders Mittens
Origin: a photograph from the January 2021 US presidential inauguration.
Meaning: looking unimpressed and dressed for the weather amid formality.
Variations: cut out and placed into other images, films and paintings.
Why it lasted: it is a cut-out, which makes it infinitely composable.

## Trollface
Origin: a 2008 MS Paint drawing by Carlos Ramirez.
Meaning: deliberate, gleeful provocation.
Why it matters: one of the earliest rage comic faces to outlive its format.

## Rage Comics
Also called: FFFFFUUUU, rage faces.
Origin: 4chan and Reddit around 2008 to 2012.
Meaning: crude serial comics built from stock faces.
Why they declined: mobile image editing and reaction GIFs replaced them, but the
faces survive individually.

## Nyan Cat
Origin: a 2011 animation pairing a pop-tart cat with a Japanese vocal track.
Meaning: early internet absurdism, endless looping.
Variations: sold as an NFT in 2021, which made it a reference point in arguments
about whether memes can be owned.

## Harlem Shake
Origin: a 2013 video format set to Baauer's track — one person dancing, then a
sudden cut to everyone.
Meaning: sudden collective chaos.
Why it matters: an early example of a format that spread through imitation
videos rather than images, which is now the default.

## Ice Bucket Challenge
Origin: 2014 fundraising campaign for ALS research.
Meaning: participation through nomination.
Why it matters: one of the few memes with a measurable charitable outcome, and
the template for later nomination-based challenges.

## Planking / Owling
Origin: around 2011, photographs of people lying rigid in unusual places.
Meaning: physical-world performance for online audiences.
Why it matters: an early physical-world meme, the ancestor of TikTok challenges.

## Karen
Origin: a name that became shorthand around 2018 to 2020.
Meaning: an entitled person, usually a woman, demanding to speak to a manager.
Criticism: widely discussed as a term that flattens into misogyny and ageism.
Variations: regional equivalents in several countries.

## Gaslighting, Gatekeeping, Girlbossing
Origin: a 2022 Twitter joke reducing three critiques to a rhythmic slogan.
Meaning: mocking corporate feminism and the overuse of therapy vocabulary
online.
Why it matters: an example of a meme made purely of other discourse.

## Corecore
Origin: a TikTok format from late 2022, collaging unrelated clips over ambient
music.
Meaning: an expression of digital overload, sometimes read as sincere art.
Why it matters: a meme format that is explicitly about the exhaustion of meme
formats.

## Grimace Shake
Origin: a 2023 McDonald's promotional drink.
Meaning: a horror-trend where creators pretended the drink caused disaster.
Why it matters: brand marketing being taken and inverted by users within days.

## Barbenheimer
Origin: July 2023, the same-day release of Barbie and Oppenheimer.
Meaning: enthusiasm for the absurd contrast of a double feature.
Why it matters: an audience-generated marketing event that studios did not plan
and could not have bought.

## Moo Deng
Origin: a pygmy hippopotamus born in Thailand in 2024 who became a global
internet subject.
Meaning: uncomplicated collective affection for an animal.
Why it matters: part of a long line — Grumpy Cat, Doge, Noodle — showing that
animal memes remain the most durable category.

## Brain Rot
Origin: slang that surged in 2024 for content that is compulsively watchable and
worthless.
Meaning: both a complaint and a badge. Named Oxford word of the year in 2024.
Variations: "brain rot" as a genre label for deliberately incoherent content.

## Glazing
Origin: slang popularised through streaming culture around 2023.
Meaning: excessive flattery of a person or thing.
Variations: "stop glazing", used to shut down praise seen as sycophantic.

## Delulu
Origin: fandom slang, shortened from "delusional", popularised around 2023.
Meaning: wilful, self-aware optimism.
Variations: "delulu is the solulu" — the delusion is the solution.

## Aura
Origin: slang that surged in 2024.
Meaning: a running score for how impressive or embarrassing your conduct is —
"losing aura points" for social failure.
Why it matters: a rare case of slang that invents a quantified social metric.

## Chill Guy
Origin: a 2023 drawing by artist Phillip Banks of a relaxed dog in a sweater,
which went viral in late 2024.
Meaning: unbothered calm.
Note: the artist objected to the image being used for cryptocurrency schemes,
repeating the pattern seen with Pepe the Frog.

## Demure
Origin: a 2024 TikTok by Jools Lebron using "very demure, very mindful".
Meaning: self-aware restraint, used ironically.
Why it matters: an example of a phrase whose commercial value was disputed
almost immediately, raising who owns a catchphrase.

## Hawk Tuah
Origin: a 2024 street interview clip.
Meaning: a crude phrase that became a shorthand for accidental virality itself.
Why it matters: a clear case of a person becoming a meme without choosing to,
and the subsequent debate about consent in virality.
